# Cost to Serve

Cost to serve is the cost of delivering and supporting a product or service for a defined customer, segment, order, or task over a stated period. The analysis includes directly traced costs and any shared costs assigned using a documented method.

## What should you include?

Start with the decision and choose the cost boundary. For a SaaS account, useful categories may include:

| Cost category | Possible basis |
|---|---|
| Production infrastructure and storage | Measured consumption or an appropriate allocation. |
| AI inference and other paid services | Requests, tokens, or billed operations attributable to the account. |
| Support and manual operations | Time spent multiplied by a documented labor cost rate. |
| Shared service resources | A stated allocation based on relevant activity or capacity. |

Keep initial acquisition costs separate unless the analysis explicitly includes them. Distinguish one-time onboarding or migration work from recurring service costs.

Reconcile the categories so shared allocations do not include infrastructure or labor already counted elsewhere in the model.

Cost to serve is an internal analytical view. Its cost pool is not automatically identical to reported cost of revenue or to all variable costs.

## A cost-to-serve example

For a separate illustrative account-month, assume these measured or allocated costs:

| Item | Calculation | Monthly cost |
|---|---|---:|
| Infrastructure and storage | Assigned consumption | $18 |
| Paid AI operations | Usage attributable to the account | $6 |
| Support work | 0.5 hours × $40 per hour | $20 |
| Other shared service costs | Documented allocation | $4 |
| Total cost to serve | $18 + $6 + $20 + $4 | $48 |

At $100 in monthly revenue, $52 remains after this stated service-cost pool. Do not label it gross profit without reconciling the categories with the company's accounting policy. Acquisition, product development, and other excluded expenses still matter.

## How do you allocate shared costs?

Trace costs directly where practical. For shared resources, choose a driver related to consumption: processing work, storage, support time, or another relevant activity.

An equal split across accounts may be a useful first estimate, but can hide large differences in usage. Conversely, counting API calls alone can mislead when one call is much more expensive than another.

Start with the largest cost drivers. AWS's SaaS Lens explicitly discusses the tradeoff between measurement detail and instrumentation complexity. A lightweight estimate with clear assumptions can be more useful than expensive precision on a minor expense.

## How can workflow design change cost to serve?

Suppose an ambiguous import error causes customers to contact support. A redesign names the affected rows, explains the problem, and allows users to retry only those rows.

Compare completed imports, repeat attempts, and handling time. Check whether users can still reach support when the problem requires help.

In the example, reducing support effort from 30 to 15 minutes lowers allocated support cost from $20 to $10 and modeled cost to serve from $48 to $38, if the other inputs remain unchanged.

This may free team capacity without reducing payroll immediately. Separate modeled allocation, released capacity, and cash savings when reporting the outcome.

## What can an average hide?

Break down service costs by segment, pricing tier, or workflow when the differences affect decisions. A low-price account with frequent manual intervention can have different economics from a similarly priced account that completes tasks independently.

Compare like periods and workloads. One month of intensive onboarding should not automatically become the forecast for every future month.
