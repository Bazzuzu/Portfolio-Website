# Pricing Metric

A pricing metric is the unit a product uses to calculate what a customer pays, such as a licensed user, an API request, or a verified result. Choosing it determines what is counted as customers adopt the product and their needs change.

## Pricing metric vs. value metric

Pricing practitioners often use **value metric** for a charging unit intended to grow with the value customers receive. Paddle uses the term in this pricing context.

Treat that connection as something to investigate. A countable event does not prove customer value. A pricing metric is also different from a product success metric such as activation rate or task completion.

Separate three decisions:

- **Metric:** what is counted, such as a licensed editor.
- **Rate:** the amount charged per unit.
- **Packaging:** the capabilities and allowances included in a plan.

A flat subscription can coexist with a metered allowance, and a product can use more than one charging unit.

## Examples of pricing metrics

| Metric | Possible fit | Question to investigate |
|---|---|---|
| Licensed user or seat | Products where more participating users receive useful access | Does charging for each participant discourage necessary collaboration? |
| Processed document or API request | Services with measurable consumption | Can buyers predict volume, and do failed attempts count? |
| Stored data | Products where maintained storage is a meaningful resource | Can customers understand, manage, and export what is counted? |
| Verified outcome | Services with an observable accepted result | Can both parties verify the result and handle reversals? |

These are candidates, not universal recommendations. The right choice depends on the product, audience, and delivery model.

## A product-design example

Imagine a procurement tool with two candidate metrics: licensed users and completed purchase orders.

Charging for all users might affect how a company invites occasional approvers. Charging per purchase order might better reflect activity, but requires clear rules for drafts, canceled orders, and corrections.

Interview buyers and the people doing the work. Ask them to estimate next month's charge for realistic scenarios and explain which activity feels related to the value they receive. Compare those answers with actual usage and [cost to serve](/glossary/cost-to-serve.html).

Do not select a metric solely because it is easy for the billing system to count.

## How should you evaluate a candidate?

Check five practical questions:

1. **Value:** Does a larger count usually correspond to something customers find useful?
2. **Predictability:** Can the buyer estimate a bill and manage a budget?
3. **Verifiability:** Can customers inspect how the quantity was counted?
4. **Delivery economics:** How do revenue and costs change as the quantity grows?
5. **Behavior:** Does charging this way discourage the collaboration or task completion the product needs?

For [AI products](/glossary/ai-unit-economics.html), tokens can be a useful internal cost measure but difficult for some buyers to forecast. A task-based metric still needs rules for retries and acceptance.

## What is the difference from a pricing model?

The metric is the unit. The model describes how charges are organized, such as a flat subscription, graduated tiers, or [usage-based pricing](/glossary/usage-based-pricing.html).

For example, documents can be sold individually, included in a subscription allowance, or covered by prepaid credits. [Outcome-based pricing](/glossary/outcome-based-pricing.html) adds a result requirement to what becomes billable.

Changing a metric can change customer behavior as well as revenue. Test comprehension and workflow effects, and provide a clear transition for existing customers rather than assuming a pricing-page rewrite is sufficient.
