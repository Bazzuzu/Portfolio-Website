Customer Lifetime Value (LTV)

Product Strategy & Growth

Customer lifetime value (LTV or CLV) estimates the value a customer generates over their relationship with a business. The value basis must be stated: the SaaS calculation here estimates lifetime gross profit, rather than revenue alone.

LTV, CLV, and CLTV are common abbreviations for customer lifetime value. In subscription software, define whether the customer is a paying account or an individual subscriber before calculating it.

What is the SaaS LTV formula?

For a simplified subscription model with stable revenue, margin, and customer churn, and no expansion revenue:

Estimated LTV = average monthly revenue per account × gross margin percentage ÷ monthly customer churn rate.

  • Average revenue per account (ARPA): monthly recurring revenue divided by paying customer accounts under a consistent reporting definition.
  • Gross margin percentage: revenue minus cost of revenue, divided by revenue.
  • Monthly customer churn: the share of paying accounts present at the start of the month that leave during that month.

Use percentages as decimals and keep periods consistent. Monthly ARPA requires monthly churn. Revenue churn, which measures lost revenue, is a different input and should not replace customer churn in this formula.

A customer lifetime value example

Assume an illustrative account pays $100 per month, with $20 in monthly cost of revenue and a constant 2% monthly customer churn rate.

Calculation Result
Monthly gross profit: $100 − $20 $80
Gross margin: $80 ÷ $100 80%
Estimated gross-profit LTV: $100 × 0.80 ÷ 0.02 $4,000
Revenue-only LTV: $100 ÷ 0.02 $5,000

The $1,000 difference is estimated lifetime cost of revenue under these assumptions. Neither result is company net profit. Acquisition cost and other operating expenses remain outside the gross-profit figure.

At a CAC of $1,000, the gross-profit estimate gives an LTV:CAC ratio of 4:1. Use the same value basis when comparing periods or products.

Why do LTV definitions differ?

Some reports use expected revenue; others adjust for gross margin or additional costs. A dashboard's label alone does not identify the calculation. Stripe Billing, for example, documents subscriber LTV as average revenue per subscriber divided by subscriber churn.

Record the formula, customer definition, included costs, and forecast horizon beside the metric. Do not compare a revenue-only LTV with a gross-profit LTV as if they measured the same thing.

When is the simple formula unreliable?

The formula assumes a constant probability of customer churn each period, not a fixed number of departures. It also leaves out expansion, contraction, discounting, and changes in delivery costs.

At zero churn, dividing by the observed churn rate provides no usable estimate. A short period without cancellations does not establish unlimited customer lifetime. Negative net revenue churn is not a substitute denominator.

For uneven retention or changing revenue, model cohorts: groups of customers acquired in the same period. Track their retained accounts, revenue, and delivery costs by month. Separate observed results from the forecast and state how far the forecast extends.

How can product designers use LTV?

Connect the estimate to behavior the team can investigate. An invoicing product might examine whether accounts that successfully send and reconcile their first invoice continue using the product.

Test onboarding changes against task completion and retained paying accounts. Include customer fit, price changes, and service costs when interpreting results. A redesign's financial effect needs evidence beyond a modeled LTV increase.

For an AI feature, also check whether additional retained usage increases delivery costs. Revenue growth and gross-profit growth can differ.
