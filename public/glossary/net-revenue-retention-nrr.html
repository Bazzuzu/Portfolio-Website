Net Revenue Retention (NRR)

Product Strategy & Growth

Net revenue retention (NRR), also called net dollar retention (NDR), measures recurring revenue retained from an existing customer group over a defined period, including expansion and losses from contraction and churn. It excludes revenue from new customers.

What is the NRR formula?

For a fixed group of paying accounts present at the start:

NRR = ending monthly recurring revenue from that group ÷ its starting monthly recurring revenue × 100.

Monthly recurring revenue (MRR) is a normalized monthly revenue measure, not cash collected during the period. Use the same revenue definition at both endpoints.

A movement-based version, when there are no reactivations, is:

NRR = (starting MRR + expansion MRR − contraction MRR − churned MRR) ÷ starting MRR × 100.

Choose a monthly, quarterly, or annual comparison and state it explicitly. If starting MRR is zero, the ratio is undefined.

An NRR calculation example

An illustrative SaaS business starts a quarter with 50 paying accounts and $10,000 MRR. By quarter-end, five of those accounts have canceled, and the others have these revenue changes:

Movement in the starting group MRR change
Expansion from upgrades +$2,500
Contraction from downgrades −$500
Churn from five canceled accounts −$1,000
Ending MRR from the original group $11,000

Quarterly NRR = $11,000 ÷ $10,000 × 100 = 110%.

Revenue from accounts acquired during the quarter is excluded. The result means the original group generates 10% more MRR at the ending snapshot, despite losing five customers. It does not mean every customer expanded.

Label this as a quarterly comparison; it is not an annual NRR figure.

NRR vs. GRR vs. customer retention

Gross revenue retention (GRR) excludes gains from expansion and reactivation. In this example, with no reactivations:

GRR = ($10,000 − $500 − $1,000) ÷ $10,000 × 100 = 85%.

Customer retention = 45 remaining accounts ÷ 50 starting accounts × 100 = 90%.

Metric What it tracks Can exceed 100%?
NRR Recurring revenue, including expansion Yes.
GRR Revenue retained after contraction and churn, excluding expansion No under the standard definition.
Customer retention The original customer accounts still retained No.

Read NRR and GRR together. Expansion in some accounts can offset losses elsewhere; it does not remove the need to investigate those losses.

How should you handle reactivation?

Declare the rule. In the fixed-group calculation here, an account that cancels and returns during the window remains part of the original group; its ending MRR is included. An account already inactive at the starting snapshot is outside that group.

Some dashboards include a broader pool of reactivation MRR. ChartMogul's standard report, for example, includes reactivations and offers a setting to exclude them. Check the report definition before comparing it with a fixed-cohort calculation.

Do not add reactivation on top of ending cohort revenue; that would count it twice.

What can product designers learn from NRR?

Investigate expansion and contraction separately. A workspace may upgrade because more teams adopt it, while another downgrades because its workflow no longer fits.

Test whether users understand limits, permissions, and what an upgrade adds. Keep legitimate downgrade and cancellation paths usable.

NRR is a revenue measure. Price increases can affect it, and high NRR does not establish high gross margin, customer satisfaction, or a causal effect from a redesign. For AI features, review delivery costs alongside retained revenue.
